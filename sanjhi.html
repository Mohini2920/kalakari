Sanjhi

Hand-cut paper stencil art, made to decorate temple floors during evening prayers to Krishna.

Story

Sanjhi takes its name from two words — "sajja," decoration, and "sanjh," dusk — because the finished piece was traditionally revealed to devotees each evening. Legend credits Radha with the very first Sanjhi, decorating the ground where she worshipped in order to draw Krishna to her. From there it grew into a temple ritual practised in Mathura and Vrindavan, Krishna's home region, for several centuries.

Cultural significance

This was never made to be sold — it was made to be prayed with. Temple priests and devotees cut and laid a fresh Sanjhi design each evening as part of Krishna worship, depicting his childhood stories, the Raas Leela dance, gopis, peacocks and lotus motifs. The art form and the devotion behind it were the same act; cutting the stencil with care was itself considered a form of bhakti, not a craft separate from the ritual.

How it's made

An artisan hand-cuts a design into paper — originally rough paper or banana leaf, today usually handmade recycled paper — using small, specially curved scissors, rotating the sheet to carve fine detail rather than moving the blade. This stencil, called a khakha, is then laid on a floor or water surface and filled in with dry coloured powder, flower petals, mirror pieces or metal foil to complete the design. A single elaborate piece can take anywhere from an hour to over a month.

Meet the artisan

Sanjhi survives today through a small number of documented families. In Mathura, the Verma family — brothers Vijay and Mohan Kumar Verma among them — has carried the craft for several generations. Ram Soni, whose family originally came from Uttar Pradesh, continues to practise it in Alwar, Rajasthan. Why it's at risk

01

Time-intensive

A single elaborate Sanjhi design, hand-cut with small curved scissors, can take anywhere from an hour to over a month to complete.

02

Changing demand

The ephemeral, ritual version of Sanjhi — recreated fresh each evening on a temple floor — has little commercial market of its own, so what survives now leans on demand for framed, standalone paper-cut art instead.

03

Fewer apprentices

What was once practised widely across Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat now survives mainly through a small number of families around Mathura.

04

Knowledge can disappear

The Government of India has formally listed Sanjhi as an endangered craft — official recognition that very few people can still cut a stencil this fine.

A craft doesn't disappear when the last product is sold.
It disappears when the last person who knows how to make it has no one to teach.

Preservation efforts

Sanjhi received a Geographical Indication tag in 2023, formally recognising Mathura as its home. Craft-documentation and revival groups have also begun showcasing surviving artisans' work outside temple settings — as lamp shades, wall frames and paper-cut art — giving the technique a market beyond its original ritual use.

Government schemes, GI registration, craft fairs and museums all contribute to preservation. But sustaining a living craft also depends on continued demand, viable livelihoods and the transmission of skills to new artisans.
