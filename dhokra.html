Story

Dhokra is both the name of a casting technique and the name of the people who carry it — the Dhokra Damar, originally a nomadic tribe of metalsmiths who moved village to village across West Bengal, Odisha, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, casting ritual objects, lamps and ornaments wherever they stopped. Over generations, many families settled into specific regions, but the craft kept their name.

Cultural significance

Many Dhokra pieces were never purely decorative — animal figurines were cast for worship, lamps for ritual use, ornaments for adornment. The method itself is startlingly old: the same lost-wax casting used to make the Indus Valley's famous bronze "Dancing Girl" thousands of years ago is, technically, what's still happening in a Dhokra workshop today, which makes every piece a living link to one of the oldest metalworking traditions on the subcontinent.

How it's made

An artisan builds the form in wax over a clay core, encases it in more clay, then fires it so the wax drains out and molten brass takes its place. Cracking open that clay shell to free the finished piece destroys the mould completely — which is exactly why no two Dhokra pieces are ever quite twins.

Why it's at risk

01

Time-intensive

Building a wax-thread form, encasing it in clay, firing it and casting brass into the mould is a multi-day process for one piece — and the mould can never be reused.

02

Changing demand

Faster casting shortcuts that skip the wax-thread stage produce a similar look at a lower price, competing directly with the slower, fully hand-built method.

03

Fewer apprentices

Dhokra is carried by small family workshops across the Ghadwa and Dhokra Damar communities; each workshop that closes takes its specific motifs and techniques with it.

04

Knowledge can disappear

The lost-wax method is one of the oldest continuously practised techniques in South Asia — but its continuity depends entirely on someone choosing to learn it in each generation.

A craft doesn't disappear when the last product is sold.
It disappears when the last person who knows how to make it has no one to teach.

Preservation efforts

Certain regional styles of Dhokra, including Chhattisgarh's Bastar Dhokra (GI-registered in 2014), carry their own Geographical Indication tags. Government emporia and craft cooperatives across the tribal belt provide some of the only steady sales channels for artisans working far from major cities.

Government schemes, GI registration, craft fairs and museums all contribute to preservation. But sustaining a living craft also depends on continued demand, viable livelihoods and the transmission of skills to new artisans.

Quick facts

  • Named after the Dhokra Damar tribe of Eastern & Central India
  • Same lost-wax method behind ancient bronzes like the Dancing Girl
  • Mould is broken to release each piece — nothing is ever recast
  • Bastar Dhokra (Chhattisgarh) carries its own GI tag
