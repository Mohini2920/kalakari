Story

Terracotta is one of the oldest crafts still practised in India — the earliest pieces, toys, seals and small mother-goddess figures, come from Indus Valley sites like Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Mehrgarh, roughly 5,000 years old. By the Mauryan and Shunga periods, potters were already using carved moulds to add fine detail to religious and everyday figures, a technique still visible in the sharper pieces made today.

What survives now is really a patchwork of regional styles, each shaped by local clay and local hands — not one continuous tradition but many small ones running in parallel.

Cultural significance

Terracotta has never been purely decorative in India — it sits right at the edge of the sacred and the everyday. Bishnupur in West Bengal built entire temples from it; villages in Tamil Nadu still raise towering Aiyyanar guardian figures at local shrines, made and consecrated as protective deities, not sold as art. At the same time, the same unglazed clay does completely unglamorous daily work — a matka keeps water cool, a kullad adds a faint earthy taste to tea, simply because nothing sits between the liquid and the clay.

How it's made

Clay is dug locally, shaped by hand or on a wheel, and fired in an open or pit kiln rather than an electric one — the colour comes entirely from the clay's own mineral content, never a glaze. Regional styles diverge sharply from here: Bankura's horses are built up by hand without a wheel at all, Alwar's pottery is thinned down almost to paper, and Khavda's vessels are painted afterward in bold geometric patterns.

Meet the artisan

Terracotta doesn't have one face here yet — it's made by many small family workshops spread across Molela, Bankura, Gorakhpur, Alwar and Khavda, each holding a slightly different regional style. Why it's at risk

01

Time-intensive

Digging clay, hand-shaping or wheel-throwing it, and firing it in an open kiln takes days per batch, with no shortcut that keeps the same unglazed finish.

02

Changing demand

Slip-cast replicas from factory moulds copy the look in a fraction of the time, and most buyers can't tell the difference at a glance.

03

Fewer apprentices

Regional styles like Molela's plaques or Bankura's horses depend on a handful of family workshops; when a family's children move to other work, that specific style usually isn't replaced.

04

Knowledge can disappear

Each regional style is a set of hand skills and clay knowledge tied to one place — once no one in that lineage is practising, that exact style is gone, even as terracotta itself survives elsewhere.

A craft doesn't disappear when the last product is sold.
It disappears when the last person who knows how to make it has no one to teach.

Preservation efforts

Gorakhpur's terracotta animal figures carry a Geographical Indication tag, granted in 2020 and backed by Uttar Pradesh's One District One Product (ODOP) scheme, which has helped that particular style find a wider market. Alwar and Khavda's styles get regional promotion through craft fairs and state emporia, and museums preserve historical pieces.

Government schemes, GI registration, craft fairs and museums all contribute to preservation. But sustaining a living craft also depends on continued demand, viable livelihoods and the transmission of skills to new artisans.
