Toda Embroidery

Counted-thread work in red and black, made only by Toda women — nowhere else on earth.

Story

Locally called pukhoor, this embroidery belongs to one community and nowhere else — the Toda tribe of the Nilgiri hills, whose entire population today numbers under 2,000 people. It was never a separate craft made for sale; it was simply how Toda men and women dressed.

Cultural significance

The shawls carry the identity of the community that makes them — worn, not just displayed, in daily Toda life and at ceremonies alike. Because the technique was taught only within the community, mother to daughter, it's inseparable from Toda identity itself; losing the skill would mean losing something the Toda people made of themselves, not just a decorative craft.

How it's made

Working freehand on unbleached cotton, the embroiderer builds red-and-black geometric patterns by counting threads in the weave rather than following a drawn outline, and finishes each piece so cleanly that the reverse side looks almost as deliberate as the front — a detail experienced eyes look for to tell a genuine piece apart from a rushed one.

Meet the artisan

Shantha Kumari has embroidered this way for more than forty years and has been recognised for it — one of a shrinking number of Toda women still working at that level, as each generation since has had fewer people choosing to learn it.

Why it's at risk

01

Time-intensive

A single embroidered shawl can take 25 days or more of counted-thread work, with no drawn outline and nothing a machine can replicate.

02

Changing demand

Field reporting on Toda artisans describes finished shawls selling to a cooperative for around ₹4,000–5,000, while tourists later pay ₹11,000–13,000 for the same piece — leaving little of that margin with the maker.

03

Fewer apprentices

The technique has only ever been taught mother to daughter inside a population of under 2,000 people, and younger Toda are increasingly choosing mainstream education and jobs instead.

04

Knowledge can disappear

Because it's never been taught outside the community, if a fluent embroiderer stops working without training someone else, that specific knowledge doesn't survive her.

A craft doesn't disappear when the last product is sold.
It disappears when the last person who knows how to make it has no one to teach.

Preservation efforts

Toda embroidery holds a Geographical Indication tag, registered in March 2013, which protects the name and origin of the craft. A 2026 field report on Toda artisans found the tag has raised the craft's public profile without significantly raising what individual embroiderers earn from it — one shawl that sells to tourists for roughly ₹11,000–13,000 may pay the maker closer to ₹4,000–5,000 through a cooperative.

Government schemes, GI registration, craft fairs and museums all contribute to preservation. But sustaining a living craft also depends on continued demand, viable livelihoods and the transmission of skills to new artisans.

Quick facts

  • Made only by the Toda tribe — fewer than 2,000 people in total
  • No pre-drawn pattern — counted directly off the weave
  • Finished to look nearly identical on both sides
  • Never taught outside the community; GI-tagged since
