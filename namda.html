Namda Craft

Felted wool rugs, hand-embroidered — one of India's quietest crafts, now down to a handful of hands.

Story

Namda isn't woven at all — it's felted. Raw sheep wool is cleaned and fluffed, traditionally with a wooden bow called a pinjra, then spread on a jute mat, dampened with soapy water and rolled and kneaded by hand until the fibres lock together into a single dense sheet, with no loom and no thread holding it together.

Cultural significance

Namda's embroidered motifs — vines, flowers, and Kashmir's signature Chinar leaf — carry real regional identity in the pattern itself. In Saurashtra, Gujarat, namda has long served a specific and unglamorous cultural role: for generations, Darbar, Patel and Marwari families have bought it specifically as padding for their horses, which is part of why the craft survived there at all — it stayed useful, not just decorative.

How it's made

Once the wool is felted and sun-dried into a plain base, an embroiderer works chain-stitch patterns into it with an aari hook. Felting and embroidery are two separate skills that combine into one finished rug, and a maker is rarely equally practised at both.

Meet the artisan

The Namda artisans we're aware of work out of Gagodar and Mundra in Kachchh, Gujarat, supported by Khamir, a crafts resource organisation based in Kutch. We haven't yet sourced directly from a named individual artisan — once we do, their name and story will replace this note.

Why it's at risk

01

Time-intensive

Felting wool by hand — fluffing, layering, wetting and kneading it into one dense sheet — takes real physical labour before a single embroidery stitch is even added.

02

Changing demand

Machine-felted, machine-embroidered rugs are faster and cheaper to produce, and increasingly compete with handmade namda even in its home markets.

03

Fewer apprentices

By one recent count, only four Namda practitioners are still active in Kachchh — not because demand vanished, but because very few people are learning the felting and embroidery skills together.

04

Knowledge can disappear

Felting and aari embroidery are two separate skills that must both be learned; losing either one breaks the chain that produces a finished Namda rug.

A craft doesn't disappear when the last product is sold.
It disappears when the last person who knows how to make it has no one to teach.

Preservation efforts

Namda holds a Geographical Indication tag in Kashmir, and in Kutch, organisations like Khamir work directly with the small number of remaining artisans. The steady, if narrow, market for namda as horse padding in Saurashtra has also helped keep the craft economically active alongside these formal efforts.

Government schemes, GI registration, craft fairs and museums all contribute to preservation. But sustaining a living craft also depends on continued demand, viable livelihoods and the transmission of skills to new artisans.

Quick facts

  • Felted, not woven — wool fibres are pressed together by hand
  • Embroidered afterward with an aari hook, in chain stitch
  • Just four practitioners known to remain active in Kachchh
  • Long used as saddle and horse padding in Saurashtra
